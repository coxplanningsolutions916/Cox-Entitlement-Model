# The product the model builds — personas, decisions, and the three depths

Prepared 1 October 2026 from Chris's direction: start from what the client wants, build that, and let it set the data and the process. The model is the backbone of the whole entitlement engagement. It starts at the first inquiry and stays with the project; every paid step adds facts, retires assumptions, and raises the confidence of the figures. Three depths of the same model:

| Depth | Price | Who does the work | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1A — Screening** | $500 (100 free in the launch campaign) | The model, from the GIS database and public sources, then **one hour of Cox staff review** before it goes out | The baseline: every section populated to the level an agency or a board would accept as a sourced, dated, assumption-labeled screen, with a planner's eyes on it |
| **Roadmap — planner review and direction** | $2,500 | **Four hours of staff time**: senior planner reviews the 1A output, corrects it, sets the strategy and the verification plan, and holds the working session | Assumptions get owners and priorities; the recommendation becomes Cox's, not the model's; the Step 1b scope and price are firm |
| **Step 1b ISR and downstream (Steps 2–5)** | Task orders | Field screening, agency pre-consultation, studies, design, applications | Facts replace assumptions; confidence classes move from derived to published, quoted and firm; the schedule and budget tighten |

The upsell is structural, not a sales pitch: the 1A output shows every assumption it rests on, what it would take to verify each one, and what verifying it costs. The client buys verification in the order that matters to their decision.

## The personas and what each wants from the model

The four website audiences, plus the operator case that walks in the door most often. All of them are decision-makers who make a living from property and are deciding, early, whether to go forward.

| Persona | The decision they are making | The questions the model must answer | What they do with the output | Timing pressure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Investor / acquirer** (buying land or an entitled site) | Buy, pass, or reprice; what contingency items to run | What can this site carry, by right and with levers; what approvals, how long, what it costs to entitle; fatal flaws (resources, access, utilities, title); what the entitled value could be against the price; what must be verified before the contingency expires | Go/no-go memo for partners and lender; the due-diligence list for the contingency period | Inside a 30 to 60 day contingency |
| **Owner** (long-hold, land-rich, often not a developer) | Develop, sell entitled, hold, lease, or partner | What the property can carry (best use against the market, not just the zoning ceiling); what it would take to entitle; rough cost and time; who buys entitled land here; the risk of waiting (rules changing); options with trade-offs in plain language | Options comparison with a recommendation and one next step; something to take to family, counsel, or a broker | Low; needs clarity more than speed |
| **Operator / business owner** (expanding or relocating a use: indoor recreation, cannabis, RV park, cemetery, industrial tenant) | Can I do this here, what permits, how long, how much, what could stop it | Use permissibility (by right, minor or conditional permit), the permit stack and approval bodies, agency timeline, site and building compliance issues (parking, ADA, occupancy, access), fees, sequence, and the one thing most likely to derail it | A permit roadmap with a timeline and fee stack to decide whether to sign the lease or buy the building | Lease or escrow deadline |
| **Developer** (seasoned; the primary target and the one the model has to impress) | Pursue the deal and under what program and schedule; what to present to an investment committee or board; who to hire | Constraints map; base yield vs market-supported yield; the approval set with bodies and durations; the critical path with season windows; a program budget with confidence classes; the assumption register and verification plan; a risk register with change-order triggers; the consultant scope map; and a model that updates as studies come in | A board-grade package and a living model that becomes the entitlement backbone | Deal timing; wants certainty on sequence and cost more than speed |
| **Builder** (website audience; sometimes the developer's partner) | Schedule and buildability certainty before committing crews and capital | The same as the developer, read for site readiness: grading, utilities, access, and when the site is permit-ready | Confirms the start date they are pricing against | Construction schedule |
| **Broker / listing agent** | How to market the property and at what number | The entitled-value story: what can be built, what the path is, what a buyer's diligence will find first | A one-page listing narrative that survives a buyer's diligence | Listing cycle |

The developer sets the bar. If the 1A output is good enough for a developer's board, it is more than good enough for the other personas, who read a shorter cut of the same model.

## What the Phase 1A product contains (the initial product)

Every section sourced, dated, and confidence-labeled, with the AI-level stamp ("model output, not yet staff-reviewed"). Where a fact is not in hand the section says `[FACT NEEDED]` and what verifies it, rather than guessing.

1. **The decision in three lines** — what to build (the client's desired use against the best market fit), how it gets approved, what it takes (cost and time ranges with the assumption that carries them).
2. **Property record** — APN, acreage, owner of record, jurisdiction, zoning, General Plan designation, overlays and specific-plan or habitat-plan membership by map, Williamson Act status, easements of record where available.
3. **What the rules allow** — the development standards for the zone (uses, density, FAR, height, setbacks, coverage, parking), the by-right envelope, and the regulatory levers that could change it (density bonus, SB 9, ADU and JADU, ministerial paths, AB 2097 parking) with applicability tests.
4. **Fit** — the client's desired development against (a) what is allowed by right and (b) the highest-and-best-use read from the market (rents or comps, product form that pencils, realistic yield). Where the client's program and the market disagree, say so, in units and acres.
5. **Constraints screen** — FEMA flood, NWI and CARI aquatic features, topography and slope, soils, CNDDB occurrences within distance bands and the species whose habitat the site plausibly carries (the high-level biology screen: flags and what they imply for surveys, permits, and mitigation, never a determination), cultural sensitivity (records-search needed or not), utility district boundaries and service path, access and frontage. Mapped versus field-confirmed on every line.
6. **Historic aerial read** — imagery dates available, disturbance history, ponding in wet-season frames, structures that came and went (automated where imagery licensing allows; otherwise flagged for the Roadmap).
7. **The approval set** — which entitlements and permits, which bodies decide, typical durations, the CEQA pathway candidates, and whether a federal or state resource permit enters the picture (the structural dependency that changes everything).
8. **Schedule and cost ranges** — season windows, a critical-path read, order-of-magnitude Cox fees, third-party studies, and agency fees by confidence class; compensatory mitigation and anything else the project cannot yet know carried as named exclusions with drivers.
9. **The assumption register** — every assumption the above rests on, what verifies it, which Step 1b or later deliverable does that, and roughly what it costs. This is the upsell and the diligence list in one table.
10. **Go or no-go read and the three questions to answer next.**

## What the Roadmap ($2,500) adds

A senior planner's corrections and judgment on sections 1, 4, 7 and 9; the strategy (which path, sequencing, what to negotiate, what to avoid); the verification plan prioritized against the client's decision and timing; the 2-hour session; a firm Step 1b scope and price. The output is the same document, now Cox's recommendation, with the planner-reviewed stamp.

## What Step 1b and downstream add

Field screening and the aquatic-resource and species determinations; agency pre-consultation that converts the approval set from likely to confirmed; the defensible lever analysis; figure-quality exhibits; then design, CEQA, applications, and compliance. Each delivery writes facts into the project file and retires assumptions; the model re-prices and re-dates; the client sees the confidence composition move.

## What this changes in the build order

The brief's order was core, graph, generation, scenario, acquisition, watch. The product definition moves acquisition up: the 1A report cannot exist without the data layer, and the data layer is the real scope. Revised order:

1. **Core** (done): fee build, provenance, rules, windows.
2. **Data layer and acquisition for one region**: Sacramento County and its cities, Sutter, Placer. The screening engine Suzanne started (`cox-screening-tool`) becomes the acquisition front end; its FastAPI scaffold is the web entry point for the $500 product.
3. **The 1A report generator**: the ten sections above from the project file, with the client whitelist enforced and the AI-level stamp.
4. **The assumption register → Step 1b scoping**: the register prices the next task order from the estimating basis.
5. **Graph and schedule** (R2 structural edges), **scenarios**, **watch**.

Lemon Hill and Riego remain the two seeds; Grant Line (the Swing Lab) is the operator test case; a current Sacramento-area acquisition deal should be the investor test case.

## Decisions made (Chris, 1 October 2026)

1. **Persona priority:** developer first, then investor; owner and operator read the same model.
2. **The ten sections** stand as the 1A product.
3. **Delivery:** web report from the screening app plus a generated PDF.
4. **Data:** CNDDB is already licensed; Google Earth imagery licensing will be obtained as needed; market-data source still open.
5. **Pilot jurisdictions:** Sacramento County and Placer County including every city in each, then the whole state.
6. **1A is a staff product, not a fully automated app:** $500 includes one hour of staff review of the model-generated report; the $2,500 Roadmap includes four staff hours. The expert planning staff is the differentiator against automated real-estate analysis apps.
7. **Launch campaign:** 100 free 1A screens, targeted at developers and investors on LinkedIn and Google, plus direct mail to property owners.

Still open: the market-data source; the staff hour's checklist for 1A (what the hour verifies, so it is consistent across planners); the competitive review (below, in progress).

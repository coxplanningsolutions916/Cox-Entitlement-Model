## Where they stop and where Cox starts

1. **They answer "what can I build"; Cox answers "how do I get it approved, what it costs, how long, and what could stop it."** Every tool above computes zoning capacity and, in some, a pro forma. Only PermitPortal maps an approval path, and it stops at the political read. None carries the California resource-permit stack (Section 404, 401, 1602, 2081, ESA Section 7), the CEQA pathway decision, season windows, or compensatory mitigation, which are the lines that set the schedule and the budget on most California sites with any resource on them.
2. **They produce a number; Cox produces a number with its confidence and its assumptions.** The model's confidence classes, the assumption register, and the unquantifiable lines carried as named exclusions are what make the output acceptable to a board or an agency. A pro forma with a yield and no provenance is what a developer's own analyst already produces.
3. **They are automated; Cox is reviewed.** PermitPortal and Transect are the two that put a person on the output, and PermitPortal's human is a sector generalist across every US jurisdiction. Cox's staff hour is a California land-use and environmental planner who will carry the same project through entitlement. That is the differentiator Chris named, and it is why the $500 product keeps a staff hour instead of racing the apps to zero.
4. **They end at the screen; the Cox model is the project backbone.** None of the tools carries the site forward as facts replace assumptions through field work, pre-consultation, design and applications. The living model, re-priced and re-dated at every step, is the product the apps cannot copy without becoming a consultancy.
5. **Depth over breadth, by design.** Every competitor sells national or global coverage of a shallow layer. Cox pilots two counties and their cities at full depth (development standards per zone, local fee schedules with effective dates, the local parking and density-bonus rules confirmed, CNDDB-backed biology flags) and expands when the depth holds. A statewide shallow screen already exists in the market; a deep one does not.

## What to borrow

- **PermitPortal's nightly monitoring and jurisdiction scoring** is the model's Phase 6 watch, and its "predecessor chain" permit schedule is the Phase 2 graph. Worth studying their Site Risk Brief format before Phase 3 generation.
- **Deepblocks' "every figure has a source"** is the same provenance rule; Cox already enforces it at load time.
- **Transect's free mini-report from a KML** is the right shape for the 100-screen launch: a KML or an APN in, a report out, with the upgrade path visible on the last page.
- **Price anchoring.** The subscriptions cluster at $100 to $500 a month for unlimited shallow screens. Cox's $500 per site with a planner's hour and a $2,500 reviewed Roadmap sits above them on purpose; the message is a reviewed, California-specific answer per site, not a seat license.

## Risks to watch

- PermitPortal is the closest in positioning (48-hour brief, human review, go/no-go) and could add a California environmental layer by partnership. Cox's answer is depth and continuity, not speed.
- The zoning-data vendors (Zoneomics, Gridics) are potential **suppliers**, not competitors: licensed code text per zone could shorten the development-standards capture that is the real cost of the pilot.
- Any of them can produce a screen for free to a prospect Cox is courting. The free intake screen and the 100-screen campaign need to be visibly better on the first page: the decision in three lines, the approval set, and the assumption register.
